<h2>The Neath: A Subterranean Labyrinth</h2>

<p>Forty years ago, London was stolen. It did not fall to an invading army, nor was it consumed by plague. On February 14, 1862, the capital of the British Empire was violently dragged beneath the earth by a swarm of colossal bats, becoming the Fifth City in a vast, impossible cavern known as the <em>Neath</em>. This catastrophe was not an accident of nature. It was the result of a desperate bargain: Queen Victoria, the "Traitor Empress," traded the entire city for the life of her beloved consort, Prince Albert, who lay dying of typhoid. The city now sits upon the ruins of the Fourth City, a fallen empire whose bones still litter the subterranean landscape.</p>

<h3>A Geography of the Impossible</h3>
<p>The Neath is not merely a cave; it is a world unto itself. Above, the "sky" is a rocky roof studded with false-stars—luminescent fungi, or perhaps colossal glowing insects, that offer a sickly substitute for moonlight. Below and beyond, the city is surrounded by the <em>Unterzee</em>, a vast underground sea of impossible colours, patrolled by zee-monsters and dotted with islands of screaming clay and ancient, alien civilisations like the Khanate empire. Most disorienting is the "Treachery of Maps." Space folds upon itself in the dark; clocks and maps can no longer be trusted. A street you knew yesterday may lead somewhere entirely different today, and the very fabric of physical law frays the further you move from the sunlight's memory.</p>

<h3>The Macabre Economy of Death</h3>
<p>The most profound anomaly of the Neath is the fragility of the final curtain. Here, death is rarely a permanent state. A slit throat, a fatal gunshot, or a drowning is often merely a temporary inconvenience. The deceased find themselves on a slow, silent boat drifting down a dark river, from which most eventually return, gasping, to their own corpses—provided the body is still intact enough to receive them. Consequently, murder is viewed less as a tragedy and more as a public nuisance. This has birthed a bizarre subculture of "Tomb-Colonists"—the decaying, bandaged gentry who have outlived their bodies' expiration dates and now reside in dusty colonies, coughing and duelling away their endless afterlives.</p>

<h3>The Masters, the Bazaar, and the Great Game</h3>
<p>While the Traitor Empress remains sequestered in her Shuttered Palace and Parliament debates in flooded chambers, true power festers within the Echo Bazaar—a living, breathing labyrinth of commerce at the city's heart. The city is ruled by the <strong>Masters of the Bazaar</strong>: cloaked, clawed, inscrutable beings like Mr Iron, Mr Cups, and Mr Pages, who speak in high-pitched whispers and levy bizarre taxes on everything from speckled eggs to love stories. Hell maintains a formal, brass-bound embassy in the city, openly trading in souls and infernal contracts, much to the quiet consternation of the Church. Meanwhile, the strict social mores of Victorian society have begun to fray. Urchins rule the rooftops, criminals and revolutionaries plot in the shadows, and artists and academics pursue forbidden knowledge—from the burning language of the Correspondence to the dream-realm of Parabola.</p>

<h3>Madness, Ambition, and the Price of Everything</h3>
<p>In this claustrophobic underworld, morality is a luxury and secrets are the most valuable currency. Fallen London is a city of spies, honey-dreamers, and seekers of forbidden names. You can trade your soul for a night of impossible ecstasy, bribe a constable with echoes, or lose your mind to nightmares and be welcomed into the Royal Bethlehem Hotel—a sanatorium for the incurably insane. Every choice, from the style of your hat to the price of your soul, carries a heavy, often hilarious, and deeply terrible consequence. As the old saying goes: "All will be well and all manner of things shall be well." In Fallen London, that is either a promise, a threat, or the cruellest joke of all.</p>
